"Get a good night's sleep" has been standard advice for generations, whether aimed at students before a test or athletes before competition. New research asks whether it applies just as much to people making decisions in financial markets — and finds a more complicated answer than expected.

"Prior research on the role of sleep in a financial setting is inconclusive — some studies show that market outcomes, such as stock prices, are impacted by disruptions to participants' sleep, while other studies find no significant effects," said William Bazley, assistant professor of finance at the University of Kansas.

His paper, "Sleep Disruptions and Information Processing in Financial Markets," co-written with fellow KU assistant professors of finance Kevin Pisciotta and Carina Cuculiza, appears in Management Science.

## Turning a clock change into a natural experiment

To test the effect cleanly, Bazley's team needed a systematic, real-world shock to people's sleep alongside an objective way to measure financial information processing. Spring daylight saving time — which costs most of the country an hour of sleep overnight — offered exactly that, paired against analysts' forecasts of company earnings per share around the same window.

> "Factors that disrupt individuals' sleep could potentially lead to increased errors in roles requiring information processing, particularly among less experienced professionals."

The results split cleanly along experience lines: professional analysts' forecast quality held up despite the lost sleep, while nonprofessional and less-experienced forecasters saw a significant decline in both accuracy and optimism right after the time change.

## Why experience seems to matter

Bazley points to research on flight crews as a useful parallel — more experienced crews tend to handle sleep disruption better than novices, likely because experience builds tools and techniques that partly compensate for impaired cognition. The paper doesn't test other decision-making contexts directly, but Bazley says it's reasonable to expect the same experience-buffer effect anywhere information processing under fatigue matters — a note of caution less for veteran analysts than for the newer employees in that room who don't yet have the tools an experienced sleep-deprived colleague can lean on.
