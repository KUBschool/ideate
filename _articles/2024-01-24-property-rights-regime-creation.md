Businesses generally operate on a comfortable assumption: property rights are stable, backed by an entrenched set of rules and a political system that protects them. New historical research argues that stability is frequently manufactured, not given — and often by the very firms benefiting from it.

"According to organization and management theory, mostly we assume the external environment — such as a political, economic and social one that we collectively call the institutional environment — is a given," said Jun Ho Lee, assistant professor of strategy and international business at the University of Kansas School of Business. "Even in daily life we usually say, 'It is what it is.' In this paper, we challenge that conventional wisdom."

His article, "The Endogenous Creation of a Property Rights Regime: A Historical Approach to Firm Strategy and Governance Structure," co-written with Minyoung Kim, the Frank T. Stockton Professor of Strategic Management at KU, and Marcelo Bucheli of the University of Illinois, appears in the Academy of Management Perspectives.

## Legitimizing — and delegitimizing — on purpose

The paper's core claim is that private firms actively shape the institutional environment around their own property rights, either by legitimizing an existing legal arrangement that protects them or by working to delegitimize a rival arrangement that threatens them. Lee points to a well-known piece of American history as an illustration: "If you look back at the history of the United States, you can look at the collusion of the so-called 'robber barons' — Andrew Carnegie, John D. Rockefeller and J.P. Morgan — in the election of American president William McKinley." Having built vast business empires through aggressive vertical and horizontal integration, the magnates funded McKinley's 1896 campaign against Democrat William Jennings Bryan, who was campaigning explicitly against the era's big trusts.

> "The history behind a particular regime, as well as the degree of contestability and stability it enjoys, can provide us with strong analytical tools to understand why property rights are more secure in some regimes than in others."

## The same mechanism can run in reverse

The paper is careful to note the dynamic cuts both ways — private actors can also delegitimize existing property rights arrangements, not just defend their own. Lee points to post-apartheid South Africa: after Nelson Mandela's release in 1990, some members of the African National Congress's radical wing argued contracts signed under apartheid should be voided as products of a racist, undemocratic regime, while Mandela's own wing ultimately backed the 1990 Groote Schuur agreement preserving them. Both sides, in Lee's framing, were engaged in exactly the kind of institutional contestation his paper describes — just pointed in opposite directions.

## A challenge to "it is what it is"

Lee's broader argument is that any institutional environment — not just the specific historical cases studied — can be changed by an organized actor willing to contest it. That's a genuinely uncomfortable finding for businesses that prefer to treat their legal and political environment as fixed background rather than something actively shaped, defended, or attacked by firms with a stake in the outcome.
