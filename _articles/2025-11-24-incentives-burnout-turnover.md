Incentive pay is supposed to be a retention tool — work harder, earn more, stay longer. New research finds that logic can flip entirely, with firms sometimes choosing to burn employees out on purpose.

"We find that even when it seems to be against their interest, firms might choose to burn people out," said Rob Waiser, assistant professor of marketing at the University of Kansas. "Firms may knowingly incentivize employees strongly enough they're going to work themselves to that burnout point. Even though hiring somebody new and retraining them is costly, there are situations where the firm might still make that choice with intent."

His paper, "Incentives, Burnout, and Turnover: Dynamic Compensation Design with Effort Cost Spillover," co-written with Juan Dubra of the Universidad de Montevideo and Jean-Pierre Benoît of London Business School, appears in Quantitative Marketing and Economics.

## Why effort today costs more tomorrow

The model's key ingredient is "effort cost spillover" — the idea that working hard today doesn't just cost something today, it makes tomorrow's effort more costly too, the mechanism behind burnout itself. Most compensation models treat each period's effort as a fresh decision; Waiser's team built one where today's grind carries a price tag into next period.

> "You're either overincentivizing people and they're working themselves to burnout, or you're underincentivizing people and they back off and save themselves for future effort."

Once that spillover is built into the model, a stark pattern emerges: there's often no comfortable middle ground. A firm's equilibrium strategy tends to land at one extreme or the other — push hard enough that workers burn out and eventually leave, or ease off enough that they deliberately hold something back to protect their future capacity.

## What actually limits the damage

Even in Waiser's model, some levers genuinely help. Flexible work arrangements that reduce how much today's effort spills into tomorrow's cost can cut turnover and improve profits substantially — not through better intentions, but by changing the underlying math firms are optimizing against. Committing to two-period contracts in advance helps too, since it removes the firm's option to ratchet up incentives once it sees how much effort a worker is willing to give. The uncomfortable finding underneath both fixes: without them, a firm can be fully rational, fully aware of the burnout risk, and still choose to run people into the ground — because for a sales organization built on commissions and quotas, that can simply be the more profitable equilibrium.
