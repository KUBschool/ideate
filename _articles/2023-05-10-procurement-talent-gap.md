Everybody practices procurement, whether they realize it or not. New research argues the people whose actual job it is aren't being taught what the role now requires.

"It's really what we do every day," said Joe Walden, then an associate teaching professor of business at the University of Kansas. "When you go to the grocery store, you're there to do procurement. You're finding suppliers, negotiating with them on quality terms and delivery price, then receiving and paying for it."

His article, "What are we teaching our procurement students?", appears in the Journal of Supply Chain Management, Logistics and Procurement.

## A gap traced back to the classroom

The Institute of Supply Management defines procurement broadly — the identification, acquisition and management of the resources an organization needs to hit its strategic goals. Walden's paper argues that even though the definition is well understood across the profession, what students are actually taught rarely matches what the job demands once they're in it.

> "Even the top schools with the top supply chain programs in the world aren't necessarily teaching all the critical skills."

Walden's analysis compares what procurement professionals are expected to know against what hiring managers are actually looking for, then traces the resulting mismatch back to curriculum gaps in academic programs — including, he stresses, programs with strong overall reputations in supply chain education.

## What's missing from the curriculum

Walden points to quality assessment as chronically undertaught. "One of the most important parts of procurement is understanding quality. We've learned through so many experiences that the lowest price doesn't guarantee the best quality. Sometimes lowest price equals lowest quality, which is really why a lot of companies have gotten away from going to the lowest bidder and finding what they've started to call the 'best supplier.'" Ethics and negotiation round out his list of underemphasized skills — areas he argues are foundational to the job but treated as secondary, if they're taught at all, next to the more quantitative supply chain coursework. His hope for the paper is direct: get academic programs to reexamine what they're teaching before another generation of graduates enters procurement roles underprepared for the actual work.
