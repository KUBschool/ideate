Fashion buyers have one of retail's hardest forecasting jobs: committing to inventory long before anyone can see how a season actually sells. New research suggests the best predictor might not be the trained expert doing the buying at all.

"Crowdsourcing has been applied in several other industries for predictions, but crowdsourcing in fashion retail buying has not been done," said Murali Mantrala, the Ned Fleming Professor of Marketing at the University of Kansas.

His paper, "Can crowdsourcing improve prediction accuracy in fashion retail buying?", co-written with his former doctoral student Omid Kamran-Disfani, now an assistant professor at Southern Illinois University, appears in the Journal of Retailing.

## Why fashion forecasting is uniquely hard

Because fashion merchandise is often sourced from overseas, buyers frequently have to place orders at least six months before a selling season starts — long before any real signal about demand exists. "The forecasting problem of how much one should order for their stores is already challenging because it involves fashion, and there is great uncertainty about what the demand would be," Mantrala said. "But it's also accentuated because it has to be done so early, and you can't wait to the last minute to see if something similar has happened and then quickly buy the stock."

> "Crowds with more variation in income and demographics improve prediction accuracy."

Against that backdrop, Mantrala's team tested whether forecasts crowdsourced from ordinary customers — people with no professional buying expertise at all — could outperform the trained fashion buyers whose job this actually is. They did, and consistently so.

## What makes a crowd worth listening to

The research goes further than just confirming crowds beat experts — it identifies what makes a crowd's forecast reliable in the first place. Heterogeneity turned out to matter more than raw size: a crowd with more variation in income and demographic background produced more accurate predictions than a larger but more homogeneous one. Mantrala is careful to frame the finding as a support tool rather than a replacement for buyers, who still have to weigh budget, shelf space and other operational constraints a crowdsourced number can't see. "The way we positioned it so we don't get everyone fired is this is a good support for whatever a business should do," he said. "They might want to run this kind of crowdsource and see what they come up with."
