"Ecosystem" has become one of those words that means everything and therefore risks meaning nothing. "Any time more than one person is involved, people say, 'We are part of this ecosystem.' Everybody uses it to describe anything these days," said Murali Mantrala, the Ned Fleming Professor of Marketing at the University of Kansas.

His article, "Retailer ecosystem-centric strategy: A framework for orchestrating value in platform and non-platform configurations," co-written with Shankar Ganesan of the University of Notre Dame, provides a practical roadmap for retailers actually trying to build one, rather than just borrowing the language.

## Borrowed from ecology, on purpose

Mantrala is deliberate about the term's origin. "An ecosystem is defined formally by having multiple actors who are independent but interdependent. And the connection between these actors is not necessarily imposed or contractual. It is something where people voluntarily come together in their common interest," he said. "Of course, the term was borrowed from ecology, where you could imagine animal ecosystems like in the Everglades. Everything — the vegetation, the fauna, the flora — is all in balance. But who organized it? They're not partners. In fact, many of them eat each other."

> "Retailers increasingly compete not only through traditional merchandising, pricing and operations, but also through their ability to design and govern ecosystems."

That distinction matters for strategy. A traditional retail channel is something a company builds and controls end to end; an ecosystem is something it has to orchestrate among partners it doesn't fully control — which requires a different set of tools entirely.

## Three pieces, working together

The framework links three components retailers need to get right together: value proposition architecture (what distinct value the ecosystem actually creates for customers), ecosystem configuration (which partners and platforms make that value possible), and governance mechanisms (how the retailer keeps voluntary partners aligned without the leverage of a traditional contract). Mantrala points to Amazon's Marketplace and Kroger's 84.51° data platform as leading examples already operating at this level — proof, he argues, that the retailers who treat "ecosystem" as an actual strategic discipline rather than a buzzword are the ones already pulling ahead.
