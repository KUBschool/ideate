Parking is a hassle for most drivers. For a delivery job, it's a line item that can quietly reshape an entire route.

"With more people ordering online and e-commerce increasing, there will be more and more deliveries that need to be made. That's going to further exacerbate this challenge of parking," said Sara Reed, assistant professor of business analytics at the University of Kansas.

Her paper, "Does parking matter? The impact of parking time on last-mile delivery optimization," co-written with Ann Melissa Campbell and Barrett Thomas of the University of Iowa, appears in Transportation Research Part E: Logistics and Transportation Review.

## A tactical question hiding inside a routing problem

Reed's model, which she calls CDPP (Capacitated Delivery Problem with Parking), builds parking search time directly into the routing decision rather than treating it as a fixed overhead cost applied evenly everywhere. "Our research is thinking about this from a tactical perspective of how parking time should be factored in when doing the routing," she said. "And if we do include parking time, how does that change delivery routes? Companies should be interested because if they're not including it, are they really representing what their drivers are doing?"

## Urban and rural routes need different answers

The results split cleanly by environment. In dense urban areas, where customers sit close together and parking windows are short, accounting for parking time meaningfully changes the optimal route — bundling stops differently than a model that ignores parking entirely would suggest. In rural environments, where parking is more readily available, a simpler approach serving each customer individually turns out to be sufficient, with little upside to the added routing complexity. Reed frames the finding as a caution for delivery companies modeling their own operations: a routing model that's accurate in the suburbs may quietly be wrong downtown, and vice versa.

## A problem that isn't going away

Reed, now in her third year at KU when this research was published, expects the underlying pressure to keep building rather than ease. With better delivery technology has come better parking enforcement too, she notes — meaning parking will likely remain an operational headache for delivery companies rather than a problem that resolves on its own.
