Earnings calls and conference presentations get most of the research attention when it comes to corporate disclosure. Analyst and investor days — the events where companies walk investors through strategy and operations outside the earnings cycle — have gotten comparatively little scrutiny, despite being common practice. New research asks what these events are actually doing for the market's information environment.

Min Park, associate professor of accounting at the University of Kansas, authored "Analyst/Investor Days and Firms' Information Environments," published in Accounting Horizons.

## Two possible jobs an investor day could be doing

Park's research frames two distinct ways an investor day might improve the information environment: providing information that would have arrived later anyway, just earlier — and providing context that helps people interpret information that arrives afterward, at the next earnings announcement. The study tests for both rather than assuming one or the other.

## Both effects show up in the data

Analyst forecast accuracy improves around the time of these events, across both short- and long-horizon forecasts — consistent with the "information sooner" explanation. But the effect doesn't stop there: after an investor day, information asymmetry around the firm's *next* earnings announcement declines too, and analysts respond to that later earnings news faster and with better forecast quality. That second finding is the more interesting one — it means the investor day isn't just front-loading information, it's giving analysts durable context that keeps paying off at the next real disclosure event.

> "Corporate disclosure is often a sequence of events, in which the earlier context can shape how later news is understood."

## When the complementary effect matters most

The context-providing benefit isn't uniform — it's strongest when investors and analysts are already facing more valuation uncertainty, or when the firm has recently seen an adverse change in value. In other words, investor days do the most good precisely when the market most needs help making sense of what's coming.

## A disclosure tool that pays off twice

For investor relations teams weighing whether an analyst day is worth the effort, Park's findings suggest the payoff isn't just the immediate news cycle — it compounds into a smoother, better-informed reaction to the next earnings report too. For investors, the research is a reminder that a firm's information environment isn't built by any single disclosure event in isolation, but by how earlier context shapes the market's ability to read what comes next.
