COVID-19 forced abrupt, simultaneous changes on how people shopped — travel bans, stay-at-home orders, closed storefronts. New research built a model to predict what came next, using only the limited data available in the pandemic's earliest, most uncertain days.

"First off, consumers respond differently to different things depending on their location," said Michael Lash, then an assistant professor of business at the University of Kansas. "Also, what we might assume people would logically respond to, they don't always respond to — they sometimes respond in the opposite way."

His paper, "Predicting mobility using limited data during early stages of a pandemic," co-written with S. Sajeesh and Özgür Araz of the University of Nebraska-Lincoln, appears in the Journal of Business Research.

## A model built for a specific, useful distinction

Lash's team drew a clear line between retail mobility (discretionary, leisurely shopping) and grocery or pharmacy mobility (needs-driven trips that don't disappear just because a pandemic is underway). "You've still got to eat and get your medicine whether there's a pandemic or not," he said — a distinction that let the model separate genuine behavior change from trips people were always going to take regardless of risk perception.

> "The factors driving mobility in these places, at least with respect to COVID-19, are heterogeneous. They're different in terms of both what people respond to and how they respond to them."

Testing the model across the Northeast, South and Midwest, the team found meaningfully different risk perceptions and tolerances by region — not a single national pattern, but several regional ones that responded to different inputs in different ways. In some locations, upticks in searches for pandemic-related health items tracked with decreases in retail mobility, as expected; in others, the same search pattern didn't move mobility the way a straightforward model would predict.

## Built for the next disruption, not just this one

Lash sees the model's value extending well past COVID-19 specifically. "When a pandemic or something happens that drastically affects all of our lives, having a tool like this can help retail folks have a stronger idea of what to expect from a consumer," he said. "One of the big motivators for this type of work was that people didn't really know what was going to happen as far as their businesses were concerned." The underlying approach — building a usable predictive model from limited data in a fast-moving, poorly understood early period — is, in Lash's framing, the more durable contribution: a template for the next disruption, whatever form it takes.
