Scroll any retail-worker forum and shift scheduling complaints show up fast — and usually, they're less about the hours than about who else is on the schedule. New research finds that instinct is onto something real.

"Shift work is prevalent in most businesses, and yet it doesn't really fit into any of our big pillars of HR research," said Patrick Downes, associate professor of management and entrepreneurship at the University of Kansas School of Business.

His article, "A Relational View of Shiftwork: Co-scheduling With Higher Performers," co-written with KU doctoral candidate Ella Lee, appears in Human Resource Management, where it received the journal's 2023 Best Article Award.

## The math managers usually miss

Downes' starting observation is that most scheduling decisions are made from a pure operations lens. "They think, 'If I'm going to have 1,000 customers, then I need 10 employees to service those 1,000 customers across a certain period of time. Therefore, we just need 10 bodies scheduled on Thursday,'" he said. "Our point is that when you put those 10 people in there, now you've created all these social interactions — actually 45 possible pairs of social interactions. So how does that affect the business?"

> "If you work a full month with someone better than you, then the next month you're going to be almost 8% higher in terms of your sales."

## A short-term dip, then a real payoff

The study's central finding has a shape worth understanding precisely: when a sales employee is scheduled alongside a higher performer for one week, that employee's own performance is initially slightly worse. But over the following month, performance climbs roughly 2% per week — compounding to nearly 8% higher sales after a full month of that co-scheduling. Pairing with a lower performer, notably, doesn't produce the mirror-image decline; the relationship isn't simply "you become like whoever you're scheduled with" in both directions.

## A lever hiding in plain sight

Downes sees the finding as immediately actionable for any organization already collecting individual performance data. Rather than treating scheduling purely as a headcount-coverage problem, a manager with visibility into who their top performers are has a low-cost lever available: deliberately pairing developing employees with stronger ones isn't just a nice mentorship gesture, it's a measurable performance intervention — one that costs nothing beyond thinking about the roster a little differently.
