"Most people go through life dreading they'll have a traumatic experience," photographer Diane Arbus once said. New research points to an inconvenient follow-up: most people actually have one, and workplaces generally aren't built to account for what that does to the body.

"When we think about trauma, we traditionally think about refugees, veterans, victims of natural disasters. We picture the extremes," said Elizabeth Embry, assistant professor of entrepreneurship at the University of Kansas. "The reality is that 70% of the population has experiences with or is currently experiencing a traumatic event, and that has physical ramifications."

Her paper, "If the Body Keeps the Score, What Happens When You Bring the Body to Work? Exploring the Health Effects of Trauma on Human Capital," co-written with Lisa Jones Christensen and Paul Godfrey of Brigham Young University and Arielle Badger Newman of Syracuse University, appears in the special issue "Health is Everyone's Business" in Business & Society.

## A physical toll that gets dismissed as psychological

Embry's central argument is that trauma's ramifications at work are usually treated — when they're addressed at all — as a mental health issue rather than a physical one, even though the physiological effects are real and directly bear on an employee's capacity to perform. That framing gap, she argues, is exactly why the effects go so routinely ignored or dismissed in workplace settings built around output and attendance rather than the physical state a person actually brings into the building.

> "Wise employers have always known that human capital is their greatest asset, and if an employee is not able to fully show up to work, then they can't produce for the company."

## From assembly lines to desks, the body never left the job

Embry traces a shift in how employers have historically thought about employee physicality. Before modern technology, jobs demanded physical health directly and visibly — long hours on a conveyor belt, manual labor where the body's condition was the obvious constraint on output. As work moved toward desks and knowledge tasks, she argues, employers gradually shifted from "our company needs this for output" to a vaguer, less physically-grounded sense of obligation to employees present from 9 to 5 — even though the body, and whatever trauma history it carries, never actually left the equation. The paper's practical argument is that managers need to relearn that connection explicitly, since awareness of how trauma manifests physically is the necessary first step before a workplace can become genuinely supportive rather than unintentionally working against the employees who need it most.
