When a bank fails, a regulator faces a real tradeoff: bail it out to preserve short-run stability, or let it fail to avoid encouraging future risk-taking. Public debate about that choice tends to fixate on individual, high-profile decisions. New research instead asks a harder, more useful question — does a regulator's entire pattern of decisions actually track what economic theory says it should?

## Measuring a decision rule regulators never fully reveal

Trambak Banerjee, assistant professor of business analytics at the University of Kansas, co-authored "Do Financial Regulators Act in the Public's Interest? A Bayesian Latent Class Estimation Framework for Assessing Regulatory Responses to Banking Crises" with Poulami Sharma, forthcoming in the Journal of the Royal Statistical Society: Series A. The core methodological challenge is that a regulator's internal read on how severe an economic environment is — the classification that determines which decision rule it should be applying — is never directly observable from the outside.

## Two regulators, two very different patterns

The team's Bayesian latent-class framework models that unobserved classification directly, linking bank characteristics to the probability of bailout, sale, or liquidation under each inferred economic state. Applied to U.S. banking crises in the 1980s, the results split sharply by regulator. The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation's decisions closely tracked what theory recommends, stepping in primarily during periods of genuine economic and industry-wide distress. The Federal Savings and Loan Insurance Corporation showed no such discipline — it didn't systematically distinguish high-distress environments from low-distress ones, and its decisions instead appeared more responsive to political considerations than economic ones.

## A pattern that foreshadowed a real collapse

That divergence isn't just a statistical curiosity — the FSLIC's undisciplined decision pattern lines up with its actual historical trajectory: eventual insolvency and significant losses borne by taxpayers. The framework's real value, beyond this specific historical case, is offering regulators, researchers, and oversight bodies a way to detect this kind of structural weakness in a regulator's decision-making *before* it becomes fiscally catastrophic, rather than only recognizing the pattern in hindsight. The approach generalizes past banking regulation too, to any setting — healthcare policy, consumer protection, financial supervision — where the rule a decision-maker is actually following varies by an unobserved read on the underlying conditions.
