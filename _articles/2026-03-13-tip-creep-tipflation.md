The United States has, by most measures, the most ingrained — and expensive — tipping culture in the world. According to new research, it's also gotten progressively more disconnected from what tipping is supposed to reward.

"Theoretically, when you tip for a service, your tip should be proportional to the service you receive," said Rob Waiser, assistant professor of marketing at the University of Kansas. "But that's not really what it is anymore."

His article, "When Tipping Becomes a Customer Experience Problem," published in Harvard Business Review, traces how a practice once confined to hospitality has spread into airlines, medical offices and auto repair shops — and become more expensive everywhere it shows up.

## Two names for a pattern shoppers already feel

Waiser's team gives the phenomenon two names: "tip creep," being asked to tip in places that never used to ask, and "tipflation," the expected percentage climbing even where tipping was already normal. Not long ago, 15% was a standard restaurant tip; suggested defaults of 20% or 25% are now common.

> "As marketers, we teach our students the idea of customer centricity. You win in the market by doing the best job of delivering what customers want or need from you. When it comes to tipping, businesses need to go back to fundamentals."

Waiser traces the shift to a handful of forces arriving at once — payment technology that makes prompting for a tip nearly frictionless, the growth of gig work blurring the line around which jobs count as "service," and a pandemic-era wave of goodwill toward service workers that never fully receded.

## A framework for deciding when tipping still makes sense

Rather than arguing tipping should disappear, Waiser's team lays out three conditions a business should weigh before implementing, keeping, or dropping a tipping prompt: distinctiveness (customers shouldn't be asked to tip for something essential to the purchase itself), visibility (the customer needs to be able to actually observe the service and judge its quality), and proportionality (the system should let a tip scale with the service received, rather than defaulting everyone to the same suggested percentage regardless of experience). Get those three right, Waiser argues, and tipping still works the way it's supposed to — as recognition for something extra, not a surcharge nobody quite agreed to.
