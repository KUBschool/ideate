Countering a conspiracy theory head-on often backfires, giving it more attention and making believers dig in further. A media studies chapter from a KU business professor points to an eight-decade-old radio serial for a different approach — one that worked.

Colin McRoberts, associate teaching professor of business at the University of Kansas, has written a chapter detailing the 1946 radio serial "The Adventures of Superman," in which the Man of Steel confronted a thinly veiled version of the Ku Klux Klan called "The Clan of the Fiery Cross." His chapter, "A Symbol for Hope: Superman's Battle with the KKK as a Model for Effective Cultural Engagement with Conspiracy Theories," appears in the book "The Lizard People Don't Want You to Read This: Essays on Conspiracy Theory in Popular Culture," published by McFarland & Company.

## Exposing the scam, not fighting the group

"In this serial, Superman revealed what motivated the Klan: the xenophobia, the hate, the know-nothingism," McRoberts said. "My chapter is trying to convince the writers of modern mass media to consider doing the same thing and use their stories to tell what conspiracy theories actually do in the real world."

> "They made the Klan look silly, and the silliness of the Klan was what undermined its image."

The serial's strategy wasn't to portray the Klan as a fearsome enemy Superman had to defeat through force — it was to reveal the group as a hollow, mercenary operation. As McRoberts's chapter puts it, the episodes showed that the Klan "wasn't just hateful and un-American, it was a scam. Despite its pomp and circumstance, it wasn't an ancient and impressive organization — it was just a club that milked its thuggish members to make money."

## A myth about the show, and the real lesson underneath it

For years, an apocryphal story circulated that the radio serial had damaged the real Klan by broadcasting its actual secret passwords on air. McRoberts's research found that never happened — the show's real power was reputational, not procedural. It made the Klan look ridiculous rather than dangerous, which did more to drain its appeal than any exposed secret could have. That distinction is the core of McRoberts's pitch to today's media creators: conspiracy theories often thrive on being treated as formidable, mysterious, or worth taking seriously as a threat. Undercutting that seriousness — showing the shallowness and self-interest underneath, the way the 1946 writers did — may do more damage to a conspiracy's appeal than confronting it ever could.
