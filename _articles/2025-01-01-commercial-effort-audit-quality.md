A basic assumption sits underneath a lot of audit regulation: an auditor spending time on business development and client acquisition is an auditor with less attention left for the audit itself. New research tests that assumption directly against real internal firm data — and doesn't find support for it.

Will Ciconte, assistant professor of accounting at the University of Kansas, co-authored "Where Does the Time Go? Auditors' Commercial Effort, Professional Effort, and Audit Quality" with J. Leiby and M. Willekens, published in the Journal of Accounting Research.

## Testing an assumption regulation has rarely had to prove

Ciconte's team focused specifically on senior auditors, for whom commercial responsibilities are most directly part of the job, using detailed data from inside large audit firms rather than relying on external proxies for commercial activity. The research tested whether time spent on commercial effort was associated with lower total audit effort, worse audit outcomes, or higher compensation — the mechanism the standard assumption implies.

## No tradeoff — and a specific reason why

None of those relationships held up. Commercial effort wasn't consistently linked to higher pay, and — more importantly — it wasn't linked to lower audit quality either. The more interesting finding is *why*: auditors who spent more time on commercial activity also relied more heavily on technical consultations, particularly on accounting and auditing questions, and that reliance was associated with higher-quality outcomes, including more modified audit opinions where warranted.

> "Commercially active auditors maintain audit quality by using firm quality control mechanisms rather than cutting corners."

## What actually protects audit quality, if not restraint

The finding reframes what regulators should probably be paying attention to. Rather than treating commercial motivation itself as the threat and designing rules to suppress it, Ciconte's research points toward the firm's quality-control infrastructure — access to technical expertise, consultation culture — as the thing actually doing the protective work. For firms and regulators alike, that's a meaningfully different lever to pull: less about limiting how auditors spend their time, more about making sure the support systems are strong enough that commercial and professional responsibilities don't actually have to compete.
