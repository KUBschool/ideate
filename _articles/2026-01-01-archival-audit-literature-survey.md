Decades of research on how auditing actually works have accumulated one persistent, quietly consequential confusion: two things that sound similar — audit quality and financial reporting quality — get treated as interchangeable far more often than the evidence justifies.

Chan Li, C.A. Scupin Professor at the University of Kansas, co-authored "A Survey of the Archival Audit Literature" with C. Lennox and Y. Wang, forthcoming in Contemporary Accounting Research, synthesizing the accumulated research on audit markets, audit quality, and audit personnel into a single account of what the field actually knows — and where its measurement tools fall short.

## Why researchers can't just measure audit quality directly

The starting problem the survey identifies is structural: audit assurance and audit quality aren't directly observable, so researchers have always had to rely on indirect proxies. In studies of audit fees specifically, that usually means estimating a simplified relationship rather than genuinely separating the demand side (what clients want) from the supply side (what auditors charge) — a shortcut that limits how confidently those results can be interpreted.

## Quality isn't one thing, and it isn't only the auditor's doing

The survey's most important clarification is that audit quality and financial reporting quality are genuinely distinct outcomes, shaped jointly by both the auditor's work and the client's own reporting choices — yet a great deal of research conflates the two anyway. Compounding that, the survey finds wide variation in how "audit quality" itself gets operationalized across studies, with some proxies capturing actual auditor behavior far better than others.

> "Audits play a central role in supporting trust in financial reporting, yet misunderstandings about how audits are measured can lead to misleading conclusions."

## Where the evidence is strong, and where it's thin

Beyond the measurement critique, the survey documents a real shift in research attention toward audit personnel and internal audit processes — while noting that the earliest stages of an audit remain comparatively underexamined, mostly because that data is harder to get. For practitioners and regulators trying to use decades of audit research to inform real decisions, the survey's real value is less about any single new finding and more about knowing which measures in the existing literature to trust, and which to treat with real caution.
