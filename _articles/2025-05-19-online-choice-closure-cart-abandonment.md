Somewhere between picking a product online and actually completing the purchase, a lot of shoppers quietly change their mind — or just never click through. New research locates the psychological moment where that abandonment actually starts, and finds a specific design fix for it.

## The doubt that lingers after a decision is "made"

Andrew N. K. Chen, the Ronald G. Harper Professor at the University of Kansas School of Business, co-authored "Push It Cross the Finish Line — Designing Online Interface to Induce Choice Closure at the Post-Decision Pre-Purchase Stage" with Y. Lee and W. Wang, published in Information Systems Research. The research focuses specifically on the window between selection and purchase — after a consumer has chosen a product, but before the transaction is complete — where the team finds consumers frequently experience real cognitive dissonance, driven by continued comparison with the alternatives they didn't pick.

## Two kinds of cues, both effective

The team tested interface cues designed to resolve that dissonance rather than simply make the chosen product look more appealing. Direct reinforcement cues — messages affirming the choice itself — and social reinforcement cues — signals that other shoppers made a similar choice — both measurably reduced dissonance. That reduction increased what the researchers call "choice closure," a felt sense that the decision is genuinely settled, which in turn increased overall satisfaction with the purchase decision. Notably, these effects held even though the shopper still can't test, use, or consume the product at this stage — the resolution is entirely psychological, not experiential.

## Why "make it more appealing" isn't the whole answer

The finding reframes what cart abandonment is actually about for a meaningful share of shoppers: not a product that failed to sell itself, but a decision that never fully settled in the shopper's mind. Retailers lose real revenue to abandonment after a product has already been selected — meaning the more effective lever, at least at this specific stage, may not be sweetening the deal further, but directly addressing the unsettled doubt a shopper is quietly sitting with. The research also extends the broader concept of choice closure, previously studied mostly in physical retail, into digital environments — showing that decision-settling, not just decision-influencing, is something interface design can genuinely shape.
