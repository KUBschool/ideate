Open-plan offices are often praised for collaboration and criticized for noise. New research on the actual physiological effect of that noise finds the relationship is more precise, and less intuitive, than "quieter is always better."

"It's a really complex relationship when you think about how sound affects people," said Karthik Srinivasan, assistant professor of business analytics at the University of Kansas. "Some of us like quiet environments; some of us like noisy environments. We have a fair understanding from a psychological perspective, but much needs to be understood in terms of physiological effects of prolonged sound level exposure."

His study, "Discovery of associative patterns between workplace sound level and physiological wellbeing using wearable devices and empirical Bayes modeling," appears in npj Digital Medicine.

## A U-shaped relationship, not a straight line

Conducted with the U.S. General Services Administration, the study fitted 231 federal office workers in Texas and Washington, D.C. with wearable devices tracking sound exposure alongside physiological markers over the course of the workday. The headline finding cuts against the assumption that less noise is always better.

> "Higher sound levels are not good, but so are very low sound levels."

Wellbeing peaked around 50 A-weighted decibels — roughly the hum of a refrigerator — with each 10-decibel move in either direction, louder or quieter, associated with a decline. Srinivasan attributes this partly to distraction: sudden changes in sound trigger the brain's difference-detection response, the same mechanism behind a fight-or-flight reaction to a potential threat, which a moderate, steady background level can help mask rather than amplify.

## The same sound, different people

The relationship wasn't uniform across the study's participants. People with high blood pressure showed greater sensitivity to sound level changes than the average participant, while those doing computer-intensive work showed less. "The second lesson we learned was this sound level association with physiological well-being is different for different people," Srinivasan said. "We looked at various job roles and demographics." For employers designing office space, the practical implication is less about hitting one universal decibel target and more about recognizing that different teams, doing different kinds of work, may need meaningfully different acoustic environments to do their best work.
