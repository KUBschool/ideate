Some of the most eye-catching results in empirical options research come with Sharpe ratios that would make any real trading desk suspicious. New research goes back and checks whether those numbers hold up once a specific, easy-to-miss methodological problem is corrected for.

Haitao Mo, associate professor of finance and Koch Fellow at the University of Kansas, co-authored "Too Good to Be True: Look-Ahead Bias in Empirical Options Research" with J. Duarte, C.S. Jones, M. Khorram, and J.L. Wang, forthcoming in the Review of Financial Studies.

## A specific, well-known problem revisited at scale

Look-ahead bias — using information in a backtest that wouldn't actually have been available to a trader at the time — is a well-known risk in empirical finance generally. Mo's team's contribution is systematically checking whether it explains the unusually strong results reported across a number of influential options-trading studies specifically, rather than treating the concern as a general caveat and moving on.

## When the filters are the problem

The team finds that many strategies reporting extremely high returns rely on data filters that, often unintentionally, pull in information not genuinely available at the time a portfolio would have been formed. Re-running these strategies using only information that was actually feasible in real time causes the large reported returns and Sharpe ratios to largely disappear — a pattern that holds both for strategies built around stock characteristics and for strategies built around option illiquidity.

> "Very high Sharpe ratios often signal methodological problems rather than strong economic effects."

## What's left once the bias is removed

The corrected picture is considerably less dramatic, but arguably more useful: option illiquidity turns out to be only weakly related to option returns once look-ahead bias is stripped out, and only a small number of stock characteristics remain meaningfully associated with expected option returns at all. That's not a null result — it's a more honest, if less headline-friendly, account of what actually predicts option returns.

## Why this matters beyond any one paper

Academics, practitioners, and regulators all lean on empirical options research to understand risk and pricing, which makes the stakes of this kind of correction higher than a typical methods footnote. The paper's real contribution is less about any single corrected number and more about the caution it models: unusually strong reported trading performance in an academic study is itself a signal worth investigating on its own terms, not just accepting at face value — a discipline the researchers argue should be applied more broadly across empirical finance, not just to options.
