The economic cost of the opioid crisis usually gets measured in the people directly affected by it — health outcomes, treatment costs, lost workdays. New research looks at a different population entirely: the skilled, non-using workers who simply leave.

Kevin Pisciotta, associate professor of finance at the University of Kansas, co-authored "Spillover Effects of Opioid Abuse on Skilled Human Capital and Innovation Activity" with K. Cornaggia, J. Hund, and Z. Ye, published in Management Science.

## A spillover effect on people who aren't using at all

The paper's framing is deliberate: this isn't a study of opioid users and their outcomes, it's a study of the broader community effects that ripple out to people with no direct connection to the crisis at all. Specifically, Pisciotta's team asks whether widespread opioid abuse changes where highly skilled workers — the kind whose location decisions directly shape local innovation — choose to live and work.

## Skilled workers leave, and innovation leaves with them

The answer is yes, and the mechanism is fairly direct. Areas more heavily affected by opioid abuse see greater out-migration of skilled workers, including inventors and research personnel specifically. That movement tracks with real declines in local innovative activity — both in raw volume and in the economic value of the innovation that does still happen there.

> "Skilled workers responding to declines in local amenities such as school quality, safety, and overall community well-being."

## Firms follow the workers out too

The effect isn't limited to individual relocation decisions. Pisciotta's team finds evidence that firms themselves respond by closing or relocating research facilities in more heavily affected areas — reinforcing the loss of local innovative capacity rather than firms staying put and simply working around a thinner local talent pool. The pattern is strongest among the most productive inventors and in places where broader community conditions — school quality, safety, general well-being — deteriorate most sharply alongside the opioid crisis.

## A public health problem with a genuine growth cost

The throughline is that innovation depends on more than firms and capital — it depends on the quality of the communities skilled workers are actually willing to live in. That reframes part of the opioid crisis's economic toll as a long-run growth problem, not just a public health one: a community that becomes less attractive to the people who drive innovation loses something that doesn't necessarily come back once conditions stabilize. For policymakers and community leaders, the findings are also a case that addressing the public health crisis directly may carry an economic dividend well beyond health outcomes — specifically, retaining the skilled workers a local innovation economy depends on.
