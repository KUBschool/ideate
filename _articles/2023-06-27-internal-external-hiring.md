Working hard is supposed to be rewarded — but new research suggests it's rewarded unevenly, in ways that favor people already in the building.

"People will sometimes undervalue the work they've done, the knowledge they've gathered by being at a specific location and the relationship they've developed with a manager. So when making hiring decisions, we show how that quality really matters," said Jeremy Lill, associate professor of accounting and the Jack and Shirley Howard Mid-Career Professor at the University of Kansas.

His paper, "Promote Internally or Hire Externally? The Role of Gift Exchange and Performance Measurement Precision," co-written with Eric Chan of the University of Texas and Victor Maas of the University of Amsterdam, appears in the Journal of Accounting Research.

## Effort as a hiring signal

The research finds that managers, when deciding between promoting an internal candidate and hiring externally, often choose the internal candidate despite an external pool that includes more qualified applicants — because the internal candidate has visibly raised their effort as the decision approached.

> "Many people will work hard to overcome that imprecision, and managers are more likely to reward people with internal promotions."

That effect grows stronger where performance measurement is less precise — remote work and less-monitored settings among them — because ambiguity gives conscientious effort more room to stand out and be rewarded.

## Built on a two-stage experiment

Lill's team tested the theory with 138 business students role-playing as employees and managers across a two-stage hiring simulation, tracking how effort levels shifted as a promotion decision approached and how managers responded once it arrived. The pattern, he says, reflects a version of "gift exchange" theory: effort offered in good faith tends to be reciprocated, even when a more qualified alternative is sitting in the applicant pool.
