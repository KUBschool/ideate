When Say-on-Pay gave shareholders a formal vote on executive compensation, the obvious question was whether shareholders would actually use it to change outcomes. New research asks a sharper question: does the vote even need to happen for shareholders to get real leverage?

Justin Balthrop, assistant professor of finance at the University of Kansas, co-authored "The Threat of Voiced Shareholder Disapproval and the Value of Voting" with J. Bitting, published in the Journal of Financial Research.

## A vote's value can fall even as it becomes more powerful

Balthrop's team measured how the market value of shareholder voting rights changed once Say-on-Pay gave shareholders a public channel to voice disapproval. Counterintuitively, that value *declined* — voting premiums fell by roughly 14% following Say-on-Pay's introduction. The explanation isn't that voting stopped mattering; it's that companies started conceding ground before the vote ever happened, reducing how much shareholders actually needed to exercise their formal voting power to get concessions.

## The threat is more credible from some shareholders than others

That decline in vote value wasn't uniform. It was substantially larger at firms with high institutional ownership and a track record of active shareholder voting — exactly the shareholders whose threat of public disapproval a board has real reason to take seriously. A scattered, historically passive shareholder base doesn't generate the same pre-emptive pressure.

> "Pre-vote negotiations and concessions can substitute for actual voting."

## Real changes, not just symbolic ones

The companies experiencing the sharpest declines in vote value didn't just avoid an awkward public vote — they went on to make real changes: higher investment, increased dividends, and greater board independence, followed by positive abnormal stock returns in the year after their Say-on-Pay vote. That pattern is what elevates the finding beyond a market-microstructure curiosity: it's evidence that the credible threat of disapproval produced genuinely value-enhancing corporate decisions, not just paperwork.

## Making sense of a genuinely puzzling body of prior research

The finding helps resolve something that's looked odd in prior Say-on-Pay research: high approval rates, positive market reactions to the policy, and continued executive pay growth all showing up together, which seems contradictory if you assume voting outcomes are the only channel that matters. Once pre-vote negotiation is added to the picture, the puzzle resolves — shareholders may be winning concessions quietly, well before a ballot is ever counted.
