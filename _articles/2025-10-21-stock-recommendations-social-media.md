Social media has a credibility problem, but new research suggests it may also be a surprisingly reliable source for stock tips — if you know where to look.

"We find that when investors discuss analyst revisions on Twitter, it helps the market better understand and more quickly impound the information in the analyst report," said Eric Weisbrod, associate professor of accounting at the University of Kansas.

His paper, "Social Media Discussion of Sell-Side Analyst Research: Evidence from Twitter," co-written with KU doctoral candidate Matt Peterson, Andrew Call of the University of Southern California and Mehmet Kara, examines more than 50,000 analyst recommendation revisions from 2013 to 2020, finding at least one revision-related tweet during the announcement window for over 90% of them.

## Not all tweets are created equal

The price-discovery effect was strongest for tweets from more influential, more credible accounts — those linking to an underlying source document, for instance, or posted by verified users (from the era before verification was simply a paid feature).

> "The more times information is discussed in the town square, the more that all investors can incorporate the news."

That distinction matters, Weisbrod says, because plenty of social media chatter about stocks is closer to noise — or worse. The research offers one concrete way to separate signal from the crowd: discussion tied to a real, credentialed analyst report carries real information value.

## A byproduct of information barriers

The underlying dynamic, Weisbrod notes, is that analyst recommendation revisions are typically proprietary to a brokerage's own clients — which is exactly why platforms like Twitter emerged as a workaround. Retail investors facing barriers to timely access found a public square where that information leaked out anyway, for better or worse.
