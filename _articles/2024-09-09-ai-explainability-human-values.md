Most AI explainability tools generate one explanation and hand it to everyone the same way. New research asks a simple question: what if the explanation adjusted to the person reading it?

"We should be considering human input when we're making machine learning models," said Michael Lash, then an assistant professor of business at the University of Kansas. "Moving forward, if we're going to do this right — this being data science, machine learning, predictive analytics — we've got to take human feedback into account. Humans have to be part of the design process." Lash has since joined the faculty at the University of Iowa.

His paper, "HEX: Human-in-the-loop explainability via deep reinforcement learning," appears in Decision Support Systems.

## Explanations tailored to the decision-maker

The paper examines machine learning explainability (MLX), the practice of giving decision-makers a prediction-specific rationale so they can judge whether an AI system's output is trustworthy. Lash's method, HEX, builds a specific decider's preferences and expectations directly into how that rationale gets generated, rather than treating every user of the system the same way.

> "Humans paired with machines can make better decisions. But by adding in this method, by considering a human's values and expertise, the human can get feedback on the predictions or decisions made by the AI to see whether it is making these for the right reasons."

## A revolution that doesn't have to be machine-only

Testing found that incorporating humans directly into the explanation-generating process increased reliance on the system, trust in its outputs, and users' own sense-making of why a given prediction was returned — all three moving in the same direction once the explanation reflected the specific person evaluating it. Lash described AI's trajectory as "revolutionary," but the paper's underlying argument is that the revolution doesn't have to be exclusively machine-driven: building a human's own expertise into the loop, rather than automating around it, is itself part of what makes the system worth trusting.
