The cliché of an ambulance-chasing personal injury lawyer has a corporate cousin, and new research puts real numbers behind it.

"The most cynical view of corporate ambulance chasing is that any time a stock price drops or a negative press release is announced, you see lawyers chasing it. We were curious to see if that played out in the data," said Eric Weisbrod, associate professor of accounting at the University of Kansas.

His working paper, "Corporate Ambulance Chasing? Plaintiff's Attorney Marketing as a Signal of Corporate Litigation Risk," co-written with KU's Adi Masli and Matt Peterson and Steven Kaplan of Arizona State University, examines traditional and social media marketing from plaintiff's attorneys following "adverse events" — restatements, missed earnings targets, auditor resignations, management turnover, and M&A announcements.

## A selective signal, not blanket chasing

Only about 20% of the adverse events studied led to a material lawsuit — but attorney marketing activity strongly predicted which ones would.

> "Attorneys use their judgment to selectively invest in marketing in situations where there's more likely to be grounds for a lawsuit."

That selectivity is the paper's central finding: rather than indiscriminately chasing every bad headline, plaintiff's attorneys appear to concentrate their marketing where they see a real case, making that marketing itself a usable signal of litigation risk.

## Who should be paying attention

Weisbrod points to a wider audience than just investors: audit committees and boards who could be named as defendants, insurers estimating potential payouts, and regulators interested in the social-media dimension of the pattern. Maximum potential dollar losses from securities class actions reached an all-time high of $2.25 trillion in 2023 — reason enough, he says, for anyone with exposure to take the signal seriously.
