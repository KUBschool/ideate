Experience is supposed to be an unambiguous asset for a CEO. New research finds that's only true up to a point — and in the wrong market, that point arrives faster than expected and experience can turn into a genuine liability.

"At some point in time, our knowledge becomes obsolete. If we spend too long in one particular industry — like in high-tech, where the knowledge base is changing — we will become less effective at some point," said Vincent Barker, the Edmund P. Learned Professor at the University of Kansas School of Business.

His article, "CEO's industry experience and emerging market SME performance: The effects of corruption and political uncertainty," co-written with KU doctoral graduate Juan Carlos Morales-Solis, now at West Texas A&M University, and Arkangel Cordero of the University of Texas at San Antonio, appears in the Journal of Business Venturing Insights.

## An inverted U, not a straight line

Testing data from the World Bank's Enterprise Survey of emerging-market firms from 2006 to 2019, Barker's team finds CEO industry experience helps firm growth up through roughly 11 years of tenure, after which the relationship levels off — in stable, less corrupt developing countries, additional years of experience simply stop mattering much either way.

> "So you rarely see coaches be effective with the same team for [too long]."

## Where experience actively becomes a liability

The finding gets sharper in countries vulnerable to corruption and political instability: there, firm growth rates don't just plateau past the 11-year mark, they start to decline. Barker frames the dynamic through a sports analogy he returns to often in his broader research: a coach who installs a system, succeeds with it, and then can't adapt once the game changes around them. A CEO's playbook, built and proven in one set of market conditions, doesn't automatically transfer once conditions shift — and in a volatile, corruption-prone market, conditions shift faster and more unpredictably than the CEO's accumulated experience can keep up with.

## A caution against defaulting to tenure

For boards evaluating CEO candidates in emerging or unstable markets, Barker's research complicates the simple heuristic that more industry experience is always the safer hire. Past a specific point, and specifically in exactly the markets where steady judgment matters most, that experience can become part of the problem rather than the safeguard it's assumed to be.
