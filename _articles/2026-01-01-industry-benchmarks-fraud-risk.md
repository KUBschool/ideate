When an auditor is trying to spot revenue fraud before it happens, what they compare a company's numbers *against* matters as much as the numbers themselves. New research finds the comparison auditors reach for most often isn't the one that actually catches the most fraud.

Keith Jones, area director of accounting and Deloitte Professor at the University of Kansas, co-authored "Auditor Use of Benchmarks to Assess Fraud Risk: The Case for Industry Data" with J.F. Brazel and Q. Lian, forthcoming in Current Issues in Auditing.

## What auditors reach for, and what actually works

In practice, the research finds auditors most commonly lean on prior-period balances and relationships within a client's own financial data when setting expectations during fraud-risk planning. Benchmarks built from industry data, nonfinancial measures, and cash flows get used far less often. But when Jones's team examined real, confirmed cases of revenue fraud, the pattern reversed: industry-based benchmarks turned out to be the stronger predictor of the two.

> "Large gaps between a company's revenue growth and its industry's revenue growth are strongly associated with fraudulent reporting."

## Why the popular benchmark is the weaker one

The explanation isn't complicated once you see it: benchmarks built from a client's own reported data are exactly the numbers management has the most ability to influence, which makes them a shakier foundation for detecting when that same management is manipulating results. An industry comparison sits outside the company's control entirely, which is part of why it holds up better as a warning sign — it can't be quietly shaped by the same reporting decisions an auditor is trying to scrutinize.

## A simple fix that doesn't require new modeling

The practical takeaway is refreshingly low-tech: auditors don't need a more complex fraud-detection model to act on this finding, just a shift in which comparison they reach for first. For audit professionals, prioritizing industry benchmarks over client-internal ones offers a clearer, more reliable warning sign without adding analytical overhead. For regulators and other stakeholders assessing the credibility of a company's reported numbers, the finding is a reminder that the most intuitive comparison point isn't always the most trustworthy one.
