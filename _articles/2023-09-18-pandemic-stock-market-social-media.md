The stock market is unpredictable by nature. New research asks a sharper question: what happens to that unpredictability when an external shock throws the whole system into chaos at once?

"Disasters are rare, and the COVID-19 pandemic brought a new wave of uncertainty to the stock market," said Michael Lash, then an assistant professor of business at the University of Kansas. "But social media allows us to now gather a vast amount of data to examine investor sentiment." Lash has since joined the faculty at the University of Iowa.

His paper, "Impact of the COVID-19 Pandemic on the Stock Market and Investor Online Word of Mouth," co-written with KU assistant professors of business Shaobo Li and Karthik Srinivasan and Towson University's Xiaorui Zhu, appears in Decision Support Systems.

## A signal that broke exactly when it mattered most

The team found that social media word-of-mouth about the stock market roughly doubled during the pandemic — about 1.7 times the pre-pandemic tweet volume — driven in part by a wave of new retail investors entering the market. But the signal that normally made that chatter useful for prediction stopped working right when it would have been most valuable.

> "When market conditions are normal and functioning as you might expect, there's a consistency you can observe. It's a steady signal. But when the pandemic was becoming a thing, the signals were moving all over the place."

Investor sentiment based on social media stayed stable and predictive during the pre-COVID period, the paper finds, but became unreliable during the peak-COVID and recovery periods specifically — the two windows when investors most needed a working signal to lean on.

## The lesson isn't about COVID specifically

Lash frames the takeaway as a warning against complacency in disaster conditions generally, not a COVID-specific finding. "During disasters, folks who trade and institutions that trade are going to drastically need to recalibrate their models and their way of managing portfolios if they're going to aptly account for this developing catastrophe," he said. "The details that mattered throughout COVID might not matter for whatever the next thing is. But you can use social media data to account for this, particularly if you focus on the topic of conversation that's unfolding as the event does." The research builds on Lash's earlier pandemic work modeling consumer mobility from limited early-stage data — part of a broader research thread on how models built for stable conditions need to be rebuilt, not just patched, once conditions stop being stable.
