International "home bias" — investors sticking closer to their own country's markets than diversification would recommend — is a well-documented puzzle. New research finds one surprisingly mundane force that chips away at it: simply having visited a place as a tourist.

Carina Cuculiza, assistant professor of finance at the University of Kansas, co-authored "Seeing Is Believing: Tourism and Foreign Equity Investments" with C. Antoniou, A. Kumar, and L. Yang, published in the Journal of Banking and Finance.

## More than two decades of evidence across 30 countries

Drawing on more than 20 years of data spanning 30 countries, Cuculiza's team finds that countries becoming more salient and recognizable to investors through tourism attract meaningfully greater foreign capital investment — investors allocate higher portfolio weights to destinations they've visited than global benchmarks would predict.

## Familiarity, not information

The more interesting question is *why* travel moves investment, and the team tested for the obvious alternative explanation directly: maybe travelers pick up genuine, tradable information about a country's economy while they're there. The data doesn't support that. Tourism shows no association with improved portfolio returns — travelers aren't gaining an informational edge, just comfort with the unfamiliar. That points squarely to a familiarity-based mechanism rather than an information-advantage one.

> "International travel contributes to a reduction in home bias through increased foreign portfolio investment in destination countries."

## Ruling out the obvious confound

The clear risk in a finding like this is reverse causation or some third factor driving both travel and investment. Cuculiza's team addresses it from several angles at once: instrumental variables, major sporting events treated as quasi-natural experiments, health-related travel shocks, and multiple placebo tests. The tourism-investment link holds up across every approach, and it's stronger for long-distance travel — consistent with familiarity mattering more precisely where unfamiliarity would otherwise be greatest. The effect holds for both institutional and retail investors, though notably not for central banks, whose portfolio allocations follow their own distinct logic largely insulated from familiarity effects.

## A behavioral force with real capital-flow consequences

The finding adds tourism to the list of behavioral, non-informational forces shaping where global capital actually goes — alongside factors like media coverage and cultural proximity that finance research has already documented. For destination countries, it's a small but genuine argument that tourism promotion carries a financial-market dividend beyond the travel industry itself: more visitors, eventually, means more foreign investment too.
