Calibration meetings exist to fix a real problem: one manager's "excellent" might be another's "good enough," so companies bring supervisors together to jointly review and adjust ratings before they become final. New research finds the fix doesn't work as cleanly as the process assumes — because the people supplying the information being calibrated have their own incentives, too.

Jeremy Lill, associate professor of accounting and the Jack and Shirley Howard Mid-Career Professor at the University of Kansas, co-authored "Calibration in the Performance Evaluation Process" with J.C. Bol and A.B. De Aguiar, published in Human Resource Management.

## The information a supervisor chooses not to share

Lill's team found that direct supervisors frequently act strategically during calibration discussions rather than fully disclosing everything they know about an employee's actual performance. In particular, supervisors tend to limit what they share when doing so helps them avoid scrutiny of their own initial rating — meaning the committee is often calibrating against a version of the story that's already been shaped by the supervisor's own interest in not being questioned.

## Some supervisors get adjusted, others don't

That selective disclosure doesn't distribute evenly. Supervisors who face lower personal costs from resisting pushback or questioning during the meeting saw their ratings adjusted less often — and ended up with higher final ratings than supervisors facing higher personal costs, even when the underlying employee performance was comparable. The calibration process, in other words, isn't just correcting for rating inconsistency; it's also partly reflecting which supervisors are better positioned to push back.

> "Calibration does not always correct bias or inconsistency as intended and can instead reflect differences in power and incentives among supervisors."

## Why this matters beyond one meeting

Performance ratings feed directly into pay, promotions, and career trajectories, which makes this less an academic footnote than a real source of unfairness employees may sense without being able to name. For HR leaders, the finding is a case for actively designing calibration processes to encourage genuine information-sharing — rather than assuming the format itself guarantees it — and for watching for exactly the kind of supervisor-level power imbalance this research identifies as a quiet, structural source of uneven outcomes.
