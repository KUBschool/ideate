Entrepreneurs are usually described in a specific register: independent, motivated, go-getters. New research argues something less visible is often running underneath those traits for a larger share of founders than the field has accounted for.

"We continue to find evidence there is a higher prevalence of trauma in society and a higher likelihood people who have experienced trauma in the entrepreneurial community than previously understood," said Elizabeth Embry, assistant professor of entrepreneurship at the University of Kansas.

Her article, "Now you see it: using a trauma-informed lens to redirect conversations on health, human capital and entrepreneurship," co-written with Lisa Jones Christensen of Brigham Young University, appears in the International Journal of Entrepreneurial Behavior & Research.

## A response encoded in the body, not just the mind

Embry's framework centers on three trauma manifestations — hyperarousal, intrusion and constriction, colloquially "fight, flight and freeze" — and how they show up for entrepreneurs who've experienced trauma at some point in their lives. "Trauma is an exogenous event that occurs in someone's life that fundamentally disrupts their sense of agency and ability to function normally and has some significant rewiring of identity, purpose and trust," she said. Citing a 2016 Psychological Medicine study, Embry notes roughly 70% of the global adult population has been exposed to at least one traumatic event — making trauma history far closer to the norm among founders than the exception.

> "I am not saying that every entrepreneur has a trauma history. But in reality, when you look at who falls under the catchment of entrepreneurs... it's oftentimes individuals" from populations with disproportionate trauma exposure.

## Why entrepreneurship itself can be part of the answer

Embry's research frames the flexibility inherent to entrepreneurship as something that can be genuinely adaptive for people managing a trauma history, not just a risk factor to be managed around. "A big part of engaging in entrepreneurship is saying, for example, 'Hey, I'm not just a domestic violence survivor, I'm an entrepreneur. I have these new skill sets.' This gives them another way of showing up in the world that can be separate from whatever that traumatic event or situation or circumstance was," she said.

## Where the framework could actually land

Embry sees the most direct application in incubators and accelerators, which have historically focused narrowly on teaching business fundamentals. "We're saying that while you do that, take into consideration not only the knowledge of how trauma might manifest for these individuals but also how to create an environment for them where they can prepare for and manage some of these trauma responses and continue to be successful," she said — less a call to treat trauma directly than to design support structures that don't quietly work against founders already managing it.
