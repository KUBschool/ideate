A dozen years ago, the Foxconn City industrial park in China became infamous for a wave of worker suicides, prompting the manufacturer to install safety netting around the facility rather than address the underlying conditions. New research argues that the deeper problem those suicides pointed to — coerced, unfree labor inside ordinary supply chains — never actually went away.

"Like so many other things in the supply chain, the pandemic started bringing slavery to light," said Joe Walden, then an associate teaching professor in analytics, information and operations management at the University of Kansas. "People started asking more and more, 'Who and where am I buying this from?'"

His article, "Modern Day Slavery in Your Supply Chain," appears in the Advances in Social Sciences Research Journal.

## A bigger, and differently located, problem than assumed

Walden estimates 17 to 21 million workers globally live in conditions he defines as slavery: being coerced to work, unpaid for work, or held in an environment with no real choice but to work. The location surprised him as much as the scale. "I assumed that when looking for modern-day slave labor, I was going to find everything involving the sex trade," he said. "But probably three-fourths of what's going on is not in the sex trade. It's in the supply chain."

> "We need to say, 'I've checked my supply chain. We're good. My suppliers, my supplier's suppliers and my supplier's supplier's suppliers aren't involved.'"

China is a frequent offender, Walden found, but Great Britain sees the highest concentration — which he attributes largely to the country's high volume of immigration, which can make it easier for indentured servitude or slave labor to go unnoticed. His interest in the topic began after watching the EU introduce new anti-slavery legislation. "I thought, 'If they're passing laws in Europe, how big is this issue really?' It's amazing to find out it's huge," he said, citing a Gartner survey in which 70% of companies admitted having no real visibility into their own supply chains.

## A moral argument, not just a compliance one

Walden's recommendations focus on audits and supplier-of-supplier visibility as tools countries and companies already have available but underuse. But his closing framing is less about compliance risk than about historical memory: "We fought a war over slavery 160 years ago. With all the modern-day issues that keep bubbling up over the slavery issue, we owe it to ourselves" to actually verify, rather than assume, that a supply chain is clean all the way down.
