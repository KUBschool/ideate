3D printing has moved well past its novelty-item reputation, and a newer offshoot is reshaping how some companies think about manufacturing altogether: selling the design instead of the object.

"With PF strategy, you do not have to mass produce things. You can sell the product design code and let customers personalize and print the products using 3D printing services," said Nagarajan Sethuraman, then an assistant professor of business at the University of Kansas.

His paper, "Personal fabrication as an operational strategy: Value of delegating production to customer using 3D printing," co-written with Ali Parlaktürk and Jayashankar Swaminathan of the University of North Carolina, appears in Production and Operations Management.

## Two kinds of customization, two very different outcomes

Sethuraman's team distinguishes between vertical customization (the quality and type of materials used) and horizontal customization (size, shape and color) — and finds the two behave completely differently as a business model. "We find that extracting value from personal fabrication through vertical customization is very difficult, whereas extracting value from horizontal customization is very much a feasible thing," he said.

> "There is negligible cost of the changeover as incurred in a typical manufacturing process. This is the key benefit: the flexibility."

The core advantage, Sethuraman explains, is that a 3D printer can switch from producing one shape to a completely different one with essentially no retooling cost — the same flexibility that lets a hobbyist print a PowerPoint clicker one day and a computer mouse shape the next. That flexibility is real. What's harder, the paper finds, is a firm actually charging for it: when a company customizes a product and sells it directly, the customization itself justifies a higher price, the way a personalized Build-A-Bear does. When the company instead hands the customer the design and lets them do the customizing, capturing that same value back as revenue is a much less straightforward problem.

## A real strategy, with real limits

Sethuraman is candid about where the model doesn't work yet. 3D printing remains slow, with production time scaling up sharply for larger items — ruling out true mass production for the foreseeable future, whatever "Star Trek replicator" comparisons might suggest. The strategy's real near-term home is products where horizontal customization is what customers actually want: not higher-grade materials, but a shape, size or color that's genuinely theirs.
