In the U.S. public company audit market, regulators have long worried that a lack of competition could let audit quality slide. A new working paper finds that worry may be overstated — and in some cases, backwards.

"We don't find any evidence that a lack of competition is problematic. It turns out that auditors who appear to be operating in less competitive markets are more efficient and more effective," said Will Ciconte, assistant professor of accounting at the University of Kansas.

His paper, "Profit persistence in the U.S. audit market," co-written with Andrew Kitto of the University of Massachusetts Amherst, has been accepted to the Journal of Accounting Research. It studies profit persistence — how "sticky" an audit office's profits are over time — using proprietary data on auditor realization rates.

## Why sticky profits aren't necessarily a red flag

Most large companies rely on one of the Big Four firms, in part because switching auditors is genuinely costly: developing the skills, processes and client-specific knowledge to do the work well takes time, and that investment creates a real barrier for a competitor trying to win the business.

> "We interpret the evidence as suggesting auditors with persistent profits are just providing better audits."

Ciconte's team measured competition using abnormal profitability from one year to the next, then tested whether that measure related to auditor effort and quality — hours worked, financial statement restatements, PCAOB inspection findings, and discretionary accruals.

## A caution against fixing what isn't broken

The findings suggest offices with more persistent profits invest that advantage in quality rather than simply extracting rents. For a regulator weighing changes to how the audit market is structured, Ciconte's team sees a clear implication: any changes should be made cautiously, and evidence of an actual problem should come first.
