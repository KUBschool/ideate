Families saving for college through a state-sponsored 529 plan generally assume the plan is being run in their interest. New research finds that assumption depends on something families have no visibility into at all: the state's own budget situation at the moment the plan's contract gets renegotiated.

Justin Balthrop and Gjergji Cici, the Koch Professor in Business Economics, both at the University of Kansas, co-authored "Conflicting Incentives in the Management of 529 Plans," published in the Journal of Law & Economics.

## A hidden cost tied to state budgets, not investor outcomes

Balthrop and Cici's team found that states facing genuine budgetary pressure when renegotiating contracts with 529 program managers subsequently offer plans with higher state fees, higher underlying mutual fund fees, and weaker risk-adjusted returns than plans negotiated under budget surpluses. None of that added cost buys investors anything back — the team found no evidence of compensating benefits: no better investment menus, no greater access to low-cost index funds, no improved age-based portfolio options, no higher participation.

> "States accept contract terms that give program managers greater discretion, which managers then use to pursue their own interests, including opaque revenue-sharing arrangements with mutual fund providers."

## Why fiscal stress changes the negotiation, not just the numbers

The mechanism isn't simply that stressed states negotiate worse deals out of carelessness — it's that fiscal pressure changes what states are willing to trade away in the negotiation itself. Under budget strain, states appear to accept contract terms granting program managers more discretion, and managers use that discretion for their own benefit, including revenue-sharing arrangements with mutual fund providers that aren't visible to the families actually investing.

## The first direct evidence of its kind

This is, per the researchers, the first direct evidence connecting a state's own fiscal incentives to worse outcomes for 529 investors — extending a broader research thread on conflicts of interest in retirement and savings vehicles to show that the *sponsor's* incentives, not just the *intermediary's*, shape investment quality. That's a meaningfully different culprit than the usual suspects in savings-plan research, which tends to focus on fund managers or financial advisors rather than the state itself.

## Why this reaches beyond 529 plans specifically

As states increasingly sponsor other kinds of savings vehicles under similar regulatory structures — auto-IRA programs and other state-run retirement plans among them — the same fiscal-pressure dynamic Balthrop and Cici document here is a real risk in those programs too. The findings make a fairly direct case for stronger oversight or disclosure requirements specifically targeted at protecting household investors from a conflict they have no way of seeing coming.
