Discounted cash flow valuation depends heavily on one number that's notoriously hard to pin down: the long-term growth rate. In practice, that number often comes from a rule of thumb rather than any systematic analysis. New research tries to replace the rule of thumb with something more defensible.

Angel Tengulov, assistant professor of finance at the University of Kansas, co-authored "Valuation and Long-Term Growth Expectations" with J. Zechner and J. Zwiebel, published in the Journal of Financial & Quantitative Analysis.

## Turning a rule of thumb into a testable model

Tengulov's team set out to identify which observable firm, industry, and market characteristics actually predict a company's long-term growth, and to test whether a statistical prediction framework built on those characteristics can generate useful out-of-sample estimates — the kind an analyst could plausibly use in a real valuation, not just a retrospective academic exercise.

## What predicts long-term growth, and what predicts its absence

Several characteristics turned out to carry real predictive weight, in both directions. Higher leverage is associated with lower long-term growth, and so is greater firm size and age — larger, older, more indebted firms tend to grow more slowly over the long run than newer, smaller, less leveraged ones. On the other side, measures consistent with stronger competitive barriers to entry are associated with higher subsequent growth, as are capital spending and other indicators of investment opportunity, along with analyst coverage and analyst-based long-term growth expectations. Indicators of industry contraction, unsurprisingly, track with weaker growth.

> "Market prices may not fully incorporate long-horizon growth information."

## The market hasn't fully priced this in either

The more striking finding is what happens when the model's predicted long-term growth gets tested against actual stock returns: firms with higher model-predicted growth go on to earn higher subsequent returns, even after controlling for the standard set of known return predictors. That's evidence the market isn't fully incorporating long-horizon growth information into prices — meaning a more disciplined growth estimate isn't just a better valuation input, it may also flag genuine mispricing.

## Bringing discipline to a number that's usually handled loosely

For analysts and investors, the practical payoff is a more evidence-based way to set an input that heavily influences valuation outcomes but has historically been handled with comparatively little rigor. For corporate decision-makers, the findings are also a useful mirror: financing choices, firm size, and investment intensity are shown here to carry real information about a company's own long-run growth trajectory, not just its near-term results.
