Beginning in 2020, most U.S. governors ordered lockdowns and mask mandates to slow COVID-19. Nearly three years later, research puts hard numbers on a tradeoff that, until now, had mostly been argued about in the abstract.

"Locking down a state is a complex, difficult decision for a governor that involves examining several tradeoffs. This is why we did not limit ourselves just to the benefit of reduction in cases of the virus," said Sajeev Nair, assistant professor of marketing at the University of Kansas. "We looked at metrics like GDP and unemployment, and, from a marketing point of view, things like consumer spending and customer satisfaction. So while there were benefits, there were also costs — and policymakers need to be mindful of weighing these aspects."

His paper, "Lockdown Without Loss? A Natural Experiment of Net Payoffs from COVID-19 Lockdowns," co-written with Gerard Tellis of the University of Southern California, Ashish Sood of UC Riverside and Nitish Sood of the Medical College of Georgia, appears in the Journal of Public Policy and Marketing.

## Both sides of the ledger, in the same study

Nair's team used the staggered, state-by-state rollout of lockdown orders as a natural experiment, comparing outcomes across states that locked down at different times. On the benefit side, lockdowns reduced infections by roughly 56% — about 480 fewer cases per million consumers per day. On the cost side, the same policy reduced consumer spending by 7.5%, GDP by 5.4%, and customer satisfaction by 2.2%, while raising unemployment by an average of 2% per state.

> "So while there were benefits, there were also costs — and policymakers need to be mindful of weighing these aspects."

Nair's team built the study around four questions: whether lockdowns actually reduced infections, what they cost consumers, whether they increased or suppressed mask adoption, and whether governors' decisions tracked medical science or non-medical factors instead.

## A decision governors had to make without this data

At the time governors were making these calls in real time, none of this side-by-side accounting existed yet — decisions were being made under uncertainty about both the medical and economic sides of the ledger simultaneously. Nair's framing is not that lockdowns were the wrong call, but that treating infection reduction as the only outcome that mattered was always going to be an incomplete picture. For the next public health emergency that calls for the same kind of decision, having both sides of the tradeoff quantified in advance — rather than reconstructed years later — is exactly the gap this research is aimed at closing.
