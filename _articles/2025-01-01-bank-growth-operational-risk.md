When regulators and researchers assess whether a bank is taking on too much risk, the focus usually lands on lending decisions and market exposure. New research points to a different, less-examined source of danger: how fast the bank is growing in the first place.

Atanas Mihov, associate professor of finance and Capitol Federal Fellow at the University of Kansas, co-authored "Haste Makes Waste: Banking Organization Growth and Operational Risk" with W.S. Frame and P. McLemore, forthcoming in the Review of Corporate Finance Studies.

## Growth itself as a risk factor

Mihov's team focused specifically on large banking organizations and asked whether the *pace* of growth — independent of what a bank is growing into — predicts operational trouble: failures in internal processes, people, and systems, as distinct from credit or market risk. The answer is yes. Faster-growing banks incur higher operational losses relative to their size than slower-growing peers.

## Not all growth is equally risky

The relationship isn't uniform across growth types. It's strongest when growth comes from expanding core banking activities — lending and deposit-taking — or from mergers and acquisitions, the kinds of growth that most directly strain existing operational infrastructure and integration capacity. Certain categories of operational failure show up more than others in fast-growing banks specifically: failures in meeting customer obligations, and weaknesses in business practices.

> "Rapid growth may create strains that increase the risk of internal breakdowns, even when growth appears successful on the surface."

## The tail risk is where it gets more dangerous

Beyond the average effect, faster-growing banks face a meaningfully higher likelihood of large, infrequent loss events — the kind of rare but severe operational failure that's especially hard to manage precisely because it's rare. And the pattern compounds under stress: banks that grew aggressively in the past tend to experience *worse* operational outcomes than their peers once financial conditions actually deteriorate, suggesting the strain from earlier rapid growth doesn't fully resolve on its own — it resurfaces exactly when a bank can least afford it.

## A risk that looks fine until it doesn't

The core caution here is that operational fragility from rapid growth is largely invisible in the metrics a bank typically reports as success — assets under management, market share, revenue growth all look healthy while the underlying strain builds. For bank managers overseeing expansion, the findings argue for treating growth pace itself as a risk factor requiring its own internal controls, not just a business outcome to celebrate. For regulators monitoring financial stability, the results offer a concrete, measurable signal — growth rate — worth weighing alongside the traditional indicators of bank soundness.
