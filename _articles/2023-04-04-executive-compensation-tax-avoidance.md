The line between tax avoidance and tax evasion is thin, and new research finds that where a company lands on it is shaped — in part — by how its CEO gets paid.

"Some companies like dancing on that line — like tip-tapping all around it," said Mehmet Kara, then an assistant professor of accounting at the University of Kansas, of the paper "Equity incentives and conforming tax avoidance," co-written with Michael Mayberry and Scott Rane of the University of Florida and published in Contemporary Accounting Research. Kara has since joined the faculty at another university.

The paper distinguishes "conforming" tax avoidance — which lowers both the numbers reported to the IRS and the profits shown to shareholders — from "nonconforming" avoidance, which uses loopholes to lower the tax bill without lowering reported profits.

## Risk appetite, not just tax strategy

The research finds that the risk-incentivizing component of stock option compensation (known as vega) is positively associated with conforming tax avoidance, while the value-creation component (delta) pushes the other way — and that the delta relationship is driven mainly by risk aversion rather than a genuine tradeoff between avoiding taxes and creating value.

> "One unintended consequence of executives' compensation contract design is lower profits, and this leads to lower taxes paid."

That's the paper's core warning for shareholders focused purely on maximizing profit: a compensation contract built to incentivize risk-taking can, as a side effect, push a CEO toward tax strategies that quietly shrink the numbers shareholders actually see.

## A reason to read the fine print

For a shareholder skeptical of how much a CEO's pay package actually matters, the research offers a concrete mechanism connecting compensation design to a specific, measurable financial outcome — reason enough, the study suggests, to look past headline pay figures and into how a contract is actually structured.
