Roughly 4.8 billion people worldwide now use social media, spending more than two hours a day on it. New research models what a platform's profit motive alone — with no ideology or malice required — can do to what those billions of people see.

"It is of interest to social media companies to nudge users in such a way that their engagement level increases, but as a result, echo chambers are created and the level of polarization increases," said Debabrata Dey, the Davis Area Director of Analytics, Information, and Operations and the Ronald G. Harper Professor of Artificial Intelligence and Information Systems at the University of Kansas.

His paper, "Polarization or Bias: Take Your Click on Social Media," co-written with Atanu Lahiri of the University of Texas at Dallas and Rajiv Mukherjee of Texas A&M University, appears in the Journal of the Association for Information Systems.

## Building an economic model, not just observing behavior

"Since economics is a subject of incentives, we thought that an economic tool will be useful in understanding if there is any motivation for a social media platform to create polarization or bias," Dey said. Prior field studies had documented polarization within platforms at a granular level, but hadn't connected it back to the underlying profit incentive at a macro level — the gap Dey's team set out to fill with a formal microeconomic model of a platform's user-targeting strategy.

> "In this study, we create a robust, quantitative framework of how the echo chambers are created because of a platform's nudging strategy."

## Bias and polarization as substitutes, not separate problems

The model's central finding is a kind of duality: both bias (skewing what individual users see) and polarization (deepening the gap between groups of users) can add to a platform's profit, and a platform can often substitute one for the other while protecting its bottom line. That has a direct, uncomfortable implication for regulation — "if you want to cut down on polarization, and if you put some penalty on the level of polarization that the social media platform creates, it will then shift to bias to get that extra profit," Dey said. A policy aimed narrowly at one symptom, in other words, may just relocate the underlying incentive rather than remove it — a reason, Dey's model suggests, for regulators to think about the profit motive itself rather than whichever symptom is most visible at the moment.
