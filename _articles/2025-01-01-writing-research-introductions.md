A study can be carefully designed, rigorously executed, and still struggle to get published — not because of the research itself, but because of how it opens. A new commentary makes the case that this is a far more common failure mode than researchers tend to admit.

Mike Wilkins, the Larry D. Horner/KPMG Professor at the University of Kansas, co-authored "Writing Introductions: A Framework and Commentary" with C.E. Hogan and L.A. Myers, published in Issues in Accounting Education.

## The part of the paper doing the most persuasive work

The commentary treats the introduction as the section carrying the heaviest communicative burden in a paper — the place where authors have to convince editors, reviewers, and eventual readers that the study is worth their attention, before any of the methodology or results have been presented. Deliberately, the piece steers away from research methods or theory development entirely, focusing instead on structure, content, and clarity as their own skill to be learned.

## A structured framework, not just general advice

Rather than offering loose stylistic tips, Wilkins and his co-authors lay out a specific framework for what each part of an introduction needs to accomplish — and, just as usefully, what content tends to weaken one when it's included. The throughline is that an introduction should center on motivation, contribution, and relevance, and resist the pull toward front-loading technical detail that belongs later in the paper.

> "Writing quality is a key determinant of publication success and scholarly impact."

## Introductions change as the paper does

One of the commentary's more practical observations is that an introduction isn't something to get right once and leave alone — it evolves as the underlying paper develops, and revising it is often where a study's real contribution gets sharpened into a clearer story. The authors walk through common pitfalls that show up across drafts and offer concrete ways to tighten the narrative as a project matures, treating the introduction less as a fixed opening and more as a living summary of what the paper is actually arguing.

## Why this is a methods question, not just a writing tip

It's tempting to file this under "writing advice" and move on, but the commentary's real claim is more foundational: if a genuinely strong study can fail to land its contribution because of how it's framed, then the *communication* of research is inseparable from the research process itself, not a cosmetic layer applied at the end. For doctoral students and early-career researchers, that reframing offers a concrete roadmap. For more experienced scholars, it's a useful prompt to revisit work that might be underselling its own contribution — a reminder that even excellent research still has to make its case.
