Studying immigration's effect on housing prices runs into an immediate problem: the same local economic strength that attracts immigrants also tends to push home prices up on its own, making it nearly impossible to tell which force is actually driving what. New research finds a genuinely clean way around that problem, hiding inside an existing government program.

Jide Wintoki, dean and the Henry D. Price Professor of Business at the University of Kansas, co-authored "Highly Skilled Immigrants and Local Housing Prices: Evidence from the H-1B Visa Lottery" with E. Beracha, E. Kim, and Y. Xi, published in the Journal of Real Estate Research.

## Turning a lottery into a natural experiment

The H-1B visa program randomly selects which foreign-born, highly educated workers receive permission to work in the U.S. — a random assignment mechanism that Wintoki's team uses to isolate an inflow of skilled immigrants that has nothing to do with local economic conditions. That randomness is what lets the study separate the effect of the immigrants themselves from the economic strength that would otherwise be pulling both immigration and home prices upward at the same time.

## More lottery winners, higher home prices the following year

Areas that receive a greater inflow of H-1B lottery winners go on to experience higher home price appreciation over the following year. The effect is strongest under two specific conditions: faster overall population growth in the area, and a more constrained local land supply for new housing development — in other words, exactly the places where rising demand has the least room to be absorbed by new construction.

> "Immigrants' economic profiles, particularly their income and wealth levels, may matter considerably when assessing how immigration affects housing markets."

## A different pattern than broader immigration research has found

One of the more interesting wrinkles is what *doesn't* move: the inflow of highly skilled immigrants shows no meaningful association with local rent prices, which sets this group apart from patterns found in earlier research on broader immigrant populations. Wintoki's team reads this as evidence that an immigrant group's income and wealth profile — not immigration status alone — shapes how it moves local real estate markets, with high earners pushing more directly on home-purchase demand than on the rental market.

## What this means for investors and policymakers

For real estate investors, the finding offers a genuinely useful, somewhat overlooked signal: tracking skilled-immigration flows into a market may say something real about where home values are headed. For policymakers, the results are a concrete illustration that immigration policy — specifically, programs targeting high-skill workers like H-1B — can have measurable effects on local housing markets, distinct from the effects of immigration more broadly. The study adds a specific, methodologically clean data point to a debate that's often short on either.
