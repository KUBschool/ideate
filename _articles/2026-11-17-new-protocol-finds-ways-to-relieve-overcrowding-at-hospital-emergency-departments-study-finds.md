According to the CDC, an average of 155 million visits to an emergency department occur in the United States every year. More than a million of those patients spend at least 24 hours at the ED. This has led to an epidemic of overcrowding.

“From a patient perspective, you would think that the treatment process itself is the bottleneck,” said Suman Mallik, B. Allen and Dorothy V. Lay Professor at the University of Kansas School of Business. “Turns out it’s not.”

His article titled “[The operational effectiveness of a full capacity protocol to ease crowding at an emergency department](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/deci.70040)” examines the efficiency of the full-capacity protocol (FCP), a set of temporary hospital-wide guidelines that seek to alleviate crowding at the ED. The research finds that the FCP is effective in reducing the patient length of stay by 6.23% when activated. The article appears in Decision Sciences.

Mallik teamed with fellow KU business professor Mazhar Arikan and Eastern Illinois University professor (and KU doctoral alumnus) Lu Wang to scrutinize 75,000 observations of individual patients. They found the average length of stay in the ED was 300 minutes (five hours).

“Hospital overcrowding is measured by several things. One is the waiting time before you are taken inside. Another is the total time it takes. As far as an emergency room is concerned, there are only two decisions: Either you are discharged to home or you are admitted,” Mallik said.

But when examining the cause of long delays, Mallik said he was surprised by what an emergency room doctor told him.

“I remember his exact words: ‘I can get a patient out of the ED in three hours between 8 a.m. and 5 p.m. But if you come at night — and because those workers at the other parts of the hospital are not there — it takes five hours to get the patient out,’” Mallik said.

The professor said he assumed the problem could only be fixed by adding more personnel. But his ultimate solution involved no additional hires. Instead, it merely required more coordination of existing employees.

“After your treatment is over — which means your doctor has made the decision of either go home or be admitted — if you’re admitted to the hospital, finding an empty bed is needed. And that takes quite a bit of time, believe it or not. That after-treatment time is the biggest problem. The rest of the hospital needs to accept this patient and say, ‘I have nurse resources, equipment resources and empty bed spaces.’ But that currently doesn’t happen,” he said.

The solution addresses streamlining some key mundane tasks.

The first step is to move the patient out of the emergency room as soon as approval is granted by the attending physician. That way the patient is waiting on the inpatient floor so more beds are available in the ED. The second step involves the workers (called transporters) who move beds. During full capacity, a dedicated transporter should be assigned to the ED. 

“It requires increasing coordination. The way this hospital we observed did it is that whenever full-capacity protocol is engaged, then every employee within the intranet could see a yellow band that displays ‘FCP is on,’” he said.

“The pro for this approach is that all other inpatient units know I need to have my beds ready. But the con is it unnecessarily creates a little bit of stress and anxiety. And when you turn this on and a yellow band is there, it gets the attention of the top administrators, which the nurses on the floor don’t necessarily want.”

Given how cheap technology is becoming, Mallik said he believes a simple way to implement this is to have a display board — presumably an LCD or similar screen — that tracks the availability of inpatient beds on a real-time basis by department or by specialization. This way an ED physician can look at the board and see what’s going on in the rest of the hospital.

Now in his 19th year at KU, Mallik focuses on operations management. Within this broad domain, he looks at hospital operations as well as strategic interactions in a supply chain. 

“These are not high-tech solutions. These are common sense solutions,” Mallik said. “And if medical practitioners tell me this has given them some ideas for improvement, that is a great success.”
